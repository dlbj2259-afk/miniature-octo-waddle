Build guide · Kestrel 5 · 5-inch · 6S

Build, set up and code the Kestrel 5

This guide takes the parts from the 3D model and turns them into a quad that flies. It runs in order: solder it together, configure it in Betaflight, learn how its flight code works and write your own, then fly it. Tick steps off as you go; your progress stays in this browser.

00

Before you start

Safety
  • Props off any time a battery is plugged in on the bench. A 5-inch prop at full throttle cuts skin to the bone.
  • LiPo batteries can catch fire if punctured, overcharged or shorted. Charge on a fireproof surface, in a LiPo bag, never unattended. Store at 3.8 V per cell.
  • Use a smoke stopper (a fused power lead) for the first power-up of any new solder joint.
  • Solder in a ventilated space and wear eye protection.
Rules

At about 526 g this quad is over the 250 g line, so most countries require you to register it. In the US you need FAA registration, the free TRUST certificate and Remote ID, and an FPV pilot needs a visual observer next to them. In the EU you register as an operator and complete the online Open category training. Check your own country's rules before the first flight.

What you need besides the parts

Tools

  • Temperature-controlled soldering iron, 60 W or more, with a chisel tip
  • 63/37 leaded solder (0.8 mm) and flux
  • Hex drivers: 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 mm
  • Multimeter with a continuity beep
  • Smoke stopper, wire strippers, flush cutters
  • Heat shrink, zip ties, blue threadlocker
  • Prop wrench or 8 mm socket for M5 nuts

To fly it

  • Radio transmitter with an ExpressLRS 2.4 GHz module
  • FPV goggles on 5.8 GHz that match your VTX
  • LiPo balance charger (6S capable) and LiPo bag
  • 3 or more 6S batteries
  • Spare props, lots of them
  • A simulator (Liftoff, Velocidrone or Uncrashed) to learn to fly first

Software is free: Betaflight Configurator, ExpressLRS Configurator and ESC Configurator. For the coding section you need Python 3.

01

Build it

Build from the bottom up, the same order as the exploded view in the model. Test each stage before you cover it with the next one: a bad joint is easy to fix now and painful once the stack is closed.

Soldering

Iron at 350–400 °C. Tin the pad first, tin the wire, then hold them together and touch the iron for 2–3 seconds. A good joint is shiny and shaped like a small volcano. Dull and lumpy means cold: reheat it with fresh flux.

  1. Prepare the frame

    • Lay out every part and check it against the components list in the model.
    • Bolt the four 30 mm standoffs and the two camera side plates to the bottom plate.
    • Optional: sand sharp carbon edges with wet sandpaper. Do it wet and outdoors: carbon dust is harmful to breathe and conducts electricity.

  2. Mount the motors

    • Bolt each motor to its arm with four M3 × 8 mm screws through the 16 × 16 mm holes, wires pointing toward the centre.
    • Check screw length: the screw must not reach the copper windings. If it touches, it shorts the motor. Arm (5 mm) plus thread depth in the motor base should leave a gap.
    • Put a drop of blue threadlocker on each screw (metal into metal only).
    • Don't worry about which motor spins which way yet. You fix direction in software later.

  3. Fit the ESC and solder the motors

    • Push the rubber grommets into the ESC's mounting holes and fit it on the stack bolts with the arrow pointing forward.
    • Route each motor's three wires along the arm and cut them so they reach their pads with a little slack.
    • Strip 2 mm, tin the wires and the pads, then solder. The order of the three wires does not matter.

  4. Solder the battery lead and capacitor

    • Solder the XT60 pigtail to the big battery pads at the rear of the ESC: red to + and black to −. These need a lot of heat; let the iron recover between joints.
    • Solder the 1000 µF capacitor across the same pads. The stripe on the can marks the negative leg.
    Test: multimeter on continuity across the XT60 pins. It must not beep steadily (a short chirp is the capacitor charging). Then plug a battery in through the smoke stopper: the ESC should beep a startup tune and the stopper should stay dark.

  5. Fit the flight controller

    • Plug the ESC harness (the small 8-pin cable) between ESC and FC.
    • Mount the FC on the stack with its arrow pointing forward and the USB port reachable from the side.
    • Plug in USB now and flash the firmware (Part 2, step 1). It's easier before everything else is wired.

  6. Wire the receiver

    • Four wires: 5 V, GND, and the two signal wires crossed: receiver TX to FC RX2, receiver RX to FC TX2. See the wiring map.
    • Cover the receiver in heat shrink and stick it to the bottom plate at the rear.
    • Run the two antenna leads into protective tubes along the rear arms and zip-tie them, pointing away from carbon.

  7. Wire the camera and video transmitter

    • Camera: 5 V, GND, and its video wire to the FC's CAM pad.
    • VTX: power, GND, video in from the FC's VTX pad (the FC adds the on-screen display on the way through), and SmartAudio to FC TX4 so you can change channels from your radio.
    • Mount the camera between the side plates at 25° uptilt.
    Important

    Screw the antenna onto the VTX before it ever gets power. A VTX transmitting with no antenna can burn out in seconds.

  8. Wire the GPS

    • 5 V, GND, GPS TX to FC RX3, GPS RX to FC TX3. If it has a compass, SDA to SDA and SCL to SCL.
    • Mount it on the rear of the top plate, ceramic patch facing the sky, as far from the VTX antenna and power wires as you can.

  9. Close it up

    • Bolt the top plate on. Fit the VTX antenna mount on the rear standoff.
    • Stick the grip pad on and thread the strap through the slots.
    • Spin each motor by hand: it should turn freely with no wire or zip tie touching the bell.
    Test: props off, power up through the smoke stopper again. You should see the FC LEDs, hear the ESC tune, and see camera video in your goggles.

02

Set it up in Betaflight

Betaflight is the open-source firmware that runs on the flight controller. You don't write it; you configure it. Connect the FC by USB and open Betaflight Configurator.

  1. Flash the firmware

    Firmware Flasher tab: choose your board, pick the latest stable release, click Load firmware [Online], then Flash firmware. Reconnect when it finishes.

  2. Paste the starting setup

    Open the CLI tab, paste the block below and press Enter. It sets up the ports, receiver, motor protocol, GPS, battery alarms and switches in one go. Check the UART numbers first if you wired different pads.

    betaflight/kestrel5-cli.txt
    # ports: 64 = receiver, 2 = GPS, 2048 = VTX SmartAudio, 1 = USB MSP
serial 20 1 115200 57600 0 115200
serial 1 64 115200 57600 0 115200
serial 2 2 115200 57600 0 115200
serial 3 2048 115200 57600 0 115200
# receiver: ExpressLRS speaks CRSF
feature RX_SERIAL
feature TELEMETRY
set serialrx_provider = CRSF
# motors: DShot600 with RPM telemetry; 2207 motors have 14 poles
set motor_pwm_protocol = DSHOT600
set dshot_bidir = ON
set motor_poles = 14
# GPS and on-screen display
feature GPS
feature OSD
set gps_provider = UBLOX
# battery: warn at 3.5 V per cell, empty at 3.3 V per cell
set vbat_warning_cell_voltage = 350
set vbat_min_cell_voltage = 330
# lost radio link: fly home on GPS
set failsafe_procedure = GPS-RESCUE
# switches: AUX1 arm, AUX2 angle/horizon/acro, AUX3 beeper, AUX4 turtle, AUX5 GPS rescue
aux 0 0 0 1700 2100 0 0
aux 1 1 1 900 1300 0 0
aux 2 2 1 1300 1700 0 0
aux 3 13 2 1700 2100 0 0
aux 4 35 3 1700 2100 0 0
aux 5 46 4 1700 2100 0 0
set craft_name = KESTREL5
save

  3. Calibrate and check orientation

    Setup tab: put the quad on a level surface and click Calibrate Accelerometer. Tilt the real quad: the 3D model on screen must tilt the same way. If it doesn't, the FC is mounted rotated; fix it under Configuration › Board alignment.

  4. Bind the receiver

    Set the same binding phrase on your radio's ExpressLRS module and on the receiver (flash it with ExpressLRS Configurator, or use the receiver's Wi-Fi page). Then in the Receiver tab, move each stick: the matching bar should move. Roll, pitch, yaw and throttle must each move the right bar, centred at 1500.

  5. Check motor order and direction

    Props off

    Remove all four props before this step. Plug in a battery.

    Motors tab: tick the safety switch and raise each motor's slider a little. Each must be in the right place and spin the right way, looking down from above:

    MotorPositionSpin
    M1Rear rightClockwise
    M2Front rightCounter-clockwise
    M3Rear leftCounter-clockwise
    M4Front leftClockwise

    Wrong place: use Reorder motors in the Motors tab. Wrong direction: use Motor direction in the Motors tab, or reverse it in ESC Configurator. You don't need to re-solder either way.

  6. Modes, OSD, VTX and failsafe

    • Modes: flick each switch and check the right mode lights up.
    • OSD: drag on battery voltage, flight timer, GPS satellites and home arrow.
    • Video Transmitter: load your VTX's table from its maker, choose a channel and start at 25 mW on the bench.
    • Failsafe: set GPS Rescue return altitude to about 30 m and minimum satellites to 8.
    Test: props off, arm on the bench, then switch your radio off. The motors must react within a second (failsafe). Never skip this.
03

Code it

There are three ways to "code" a drone, from understanding what the firmware does to making it fly itself. All three are in the project's drone-model/code/ folder and run on a normal computer.

Level 1 · How flight code works

The control loop

A quadcopter can't fly without a computer. It is unstable: left alone it flips in a fraction of a second. The flight controller runs this loop 4,000 to 8,000 times a second:

GyroMeasures how fast the quad is rotating on each axis
→
FilterRemoves motor vibration noise
→
PIDCompares actual rotation to what your sticks ask for
→
MixerTurns roll, pitch, yaw into four motor speeds
→
MotorsDShot commands to the ESC

↻ the quad rotates, the gyro sees it, and the loop runs again 250 µs later

The PID controller turns the error (asked-for rotation minus actual rotation) into a correction. P pushes in proportion to the error. I adds up error over time to cancel steady pushes, like a battery that's slightly off-centre. D reacts to how fast the rotation is changing and damps overshoot. This is the core of it, taken from pid_sim.py:

code/pid_sim.py · the PID step
error = setpoint - gyro                         # deg/s: what you asked for minus what's happening
pterm = p * PTERM_SCALE * error                 # push in proportion to the error
iterm += i * ITERM_SCALE * error * relax * dt   # remember error over time
dterm = d * DTERM_SCALE * -(gyro - prev_gyro) / dt   # brake when rotation changes fast
u = (pterm + iterm + dterm) / 1000              # -1..1 share of the motors' authority

The mixer decides which motors speed up. To roll right, the left motors speed up and the right ones slow down. To yaw, the two motors spinning one way speed up and the other two slow down; the frame twists the opposite way from the props. This is Betaflight's quad-X table:

quad-X mixer
#                 roll  pitch  yaw     (Betaflight quad-X)
MIX = {
    'M1 rear-right':  (-1,   +1,   -1),
    'M2 front-right': (-1,   -1,   +1),
    'M3 rear-left':   (+1,   +1,   +1),
    'M4 front-left':  (+1,   -1,   -1),
}

def mix(throttle, roll, pitch, yaw):
    return {m: throttle + r * roll + p * pitch + y * yaw
            for m, (r, p, y) in MIX.items()}

Try it: tune a PID yourself

pid_sim.py simulates the Kestrel 5's roll axis with real physics (inertia, motor lag, gyro noise, an off-centre battery) and Betaflight's own gain scaling. The P, I and D numbers mean the same as the sliders in Betaflight's PID tab.

terminal
cd drone-model/code
python3 pid_sim.py                  # Betaflight defaults: P 45, I 80, D 40
python3 pid_sim.py --i 0            # remove I and watch it drift
python3 pid_sim.py --p 110 --d 15   # too much P, too little D
python3 pid_sim.py --plot           # chart, needs matplotlib

Each run asks for a sudden 400 °/s roll and prints a chart and these numbers. What you'll see:

On the real quad, use Betaflight's simplified tuning sliders and Blackbox logs rather than guessing. The simulator is there so the sliders make sense.

Level 2 · Talk to your flight controller

Read live data with MSP

Betaflight speaks MSP (MultiWii Serial Protocol) over USB, the same protocol the Configurator uses. msp_monitor.py sends small request frames and decodes the replies: attitude, battery voltage, current and stick positions, live in your terminal. It only reads, so it can't arm or spin anything.

code/msp_monitor.py · building a request
def encode(cmd, payload=b''):
    size = len(payload)
    ck = size ^ cmd                 # checksum = XOR of size, command and payload
    for b in payload:
        ck ^= b
    return b'$M<' + bytes([size, cmd]) + payload + bytes([ck])

# MSP_ATTITUDE (108) replies with roll and pitch in 0.1° and yaw in degrees
roll, pitch, yaw = struct.unpack('<hhh', payload[:6])
terminal
pip install pyserial
python3 msp_monitor.py --selftest            # checks the protocol code, no hardware
python3 msp_monitor.py --port /dev/ttyACM0   # Linux; COM5 on Windows, /dev/cu.usbmodem… on macOS

Close Betaflight Configurator first (only one program can use the port). Tilt the quad by hand and watch roll and pitch change. From here you can log flights, build your own ground station, or show telemetry on a screen.

Level 3 · Make it fly itself

Autonomous missions in a simulator

Betaflight is built for a human pilot. For autonomy (take off, fly a route, land on its own) you use an autopilot firmware such as ArduPilot or INAV and send it commands over MAVLink. sitl_mission.py does this against ArduPilot's software-in-the-loop simulator: it arms, climbs to 10 m, flies a 20 m square and lands.

code/sitl_mission.py · the mission
d = Drone('udp:127.0.0.1:14550')
d.mode('GUIDED')       # accept commands from this script
d.arm()                # waits for GPS lock and pre-arm checks
d.takeoff(10)          # metres
for north, east in [(20, 0), (20, 20), (0, 20), (0, 0)]:
    d.goto(north, east, 10)
d.mode('LAND')
terminal
# one-time: install ArduPilot SITL (see ardupilot.org › Building the code)
sim_vehicle.py -v ArduCopter --console --map   # starts a virtual quad
pip install pymavlink
python3 sitl_mission.py
Real hardware

Prove every mission in simulation first. To fly missions on the real Kestrel 5, flash INAV instead of Betaflight (it supports F7 boards with GPS and plans waypoint missions in its configurator), or swap to an H743 flight controller that ArduPilot supports. Autonomous flight must stay within your country's line-of-sight rules.

04

First flight

  1. Learn in a simulator first

    Spend 10+ hours in a simulator with the same radio. Crashing there is free; crashing here costs props, arms and sometimes motors.

  2. Fit the props the right way round

    Orange props go on the front motors, smoke props on the rear. Each prop's raised leading edge must face the direction its motor spins. Wrong props make the quad flip the instant it lifts off. Tighten the M5 nuts firmly.

  3. Pre-flight check

    • Big open field, nobody nearby, legal to fly, spotter beside you.
    • Battery fully charged (25.2 V) and strapped tight.
    • Goggles on the same channel as the VTX; GPS showing 8+ satellites before arming (home point gets saved).
    • Failsafe tested on the bench (Part 2, step 6).

  4. Hover in Angle mode

    Switch to Angle mode (AUX2 low), arm, and raise throttle slowly until it lifts. It should hover at around 22–25% throttle and hold level. Keep it low and close. Land when the OSD shows 3.5 V per cell.

    After landing: touch the motors. Warm is fine. Too hot to hold means noise or a bad tune: check props and screws, then look at a Blackbox log before flying again.

  5. Progress to Acro

    Practise Horizon, then Acro (AUX2 high), where the quad holds whatever angle you leave it at. Acro is how FPV freestyle and racing are flown.
